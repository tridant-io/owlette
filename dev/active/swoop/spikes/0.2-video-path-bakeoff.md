## 2. Decision rule — D3 as written, and D3 as amended

### 2.1 What plan.md D3 said

> Rule: the winner beats B by ≥ 15 ms p50 photon latency on LAN and does not lose to B at 2% loss / 40 ms
> RTT; ties go to the simpler path (B < C < A).

### 2.2 What it now says

**Amended by the owner on 2026-09-18, in response to what stage 2 measured:**

> The winner beats B by ≥ 15 ms p50 on the LAN row **OR** betters B's p95 by ≥ 15 ms, **AND** does not lose to
> B at 2% loss / 40 ms RTT. Ties go to the simpler path (B < C < A).

### 2.3 Why, and what it changes

The original rule is blind to the tail, and on this pipeline the tail is where arm B's problem lives.
Measured in one sitting, same machine, n = 150 per arm (§3): arm B's p95 is **63.1 ms** against a p50 of
15.5 ms — a p95/p50 ratio of 4.1 — while arm A's p95 is **11.1 ms** against a p50 of 9.8 ms, a ratio of 1.1.
Across nine comparable arm B runs the p95 ranged **30.7 to 82.1 ms**; across arm A's four reliability modes in
the same sitting it ranged **11.0 to 11.6 ms**. The gap is not loss and it is not the network: in the arm B
row, `packetsLost` is 0, `pliCount` is 0, `freezeCount` is 0, and `push → arrival` p95 is 2.8 ms. It is
`arrival → composition`, p50 6.4 ms and **p95 51.8 ms** — Chrome's own scheduling between a frame arriving and
a `<video>` element compositing it.

A p50-only rule scores that as a 5.7 ms difference and shrugs. In an interactive remote-desktop product a
tail that is 2–4× worse than the median is what a user sees as stutter while dragging a window, and it is
precisely what "Parsec-class" means or does not mean. The owner's ruling is that a rule which cannot see it
is the wrong rule.

**This amendment changes the answer.** Under D3 as written, nothing beats B by 15 ms p50 on the
same-machine rows — arm A's margin is 5.7 ms, or 5.9 ms on its best reliability mode — so B would survive on
the tie-break. Under the amended rule,
arm A clears the p95 clause by a wide margin and B does not survive. **If the LAN rows resemble the
same-machine ones, the amendment is the difference between recommending B and recommending A.** That is
stated here rather than buried, because a rule changed after seeing the data has to be defensible on its
reasoning and not on its result: the reasoning is that a 4:1 tail ratio with zero packet loss is a product
defect, and the original rule could not express it. It is recorded before the rows that decide the gate exist
(§9, §10), so it cannot be tuned to them.

## 4. Applying the amended rule to the rows that exist

**These are same-machine rows. They rank arms. They are never a product latency (0.1 §2.2), and the rule is
formally evaluated on the LAN row, which does not exist yet.** What follows is the rule applied to the
ranking rows, so that the shape of the answer is on record before §9 and §10 run.

| clause | arm A | arm C |
|---|---|---|
| beats B by ≥ 15 ms **p50** | **no** — 5.7 ms (9.8 vs 15.5); 5.9 ms on arm A's best mode | **no** — loses by 3.7 ms (19.1 vs 15.5) |
| betters B's **p95** by ≥ 15 ms | **yes** — 52.0 ms (11.1 vs 63.1) | **yes** at this sitting — 36.5 ms (26.6 vs 63.1) |
| …and against B's **best observed** p95 (30.7 ms, `pacer-off`, n = 150) | **yes** — 19.6 ms | **no** — 4.1 ms |
| does not lose to B at 2% loss / 40 ms RTT | **PENDING [human]** | **PENDING [human]** |

Arm B's p95 is not a stable number: across nine comparable same-machine runs (all H.264, BWE off, n = 150 or
151) it was 30.7, 31.5, 31.7, 31.9, 47.7, 63.1, 64.8, 64.9 and 82.1 ms. Arm A clears the 15 ms p95 margin
against **every one of those nine**, including the best. Arm C clears it against the median but not against
the best, so **arm C does not pass the amended rule robustly** and, being the middle arm on the tie-break
ladder, has no other route in. Arm C is out on these rows.

Between A and B the first clause now turns entirely on the second: the p95 clause is met on the ranking rows,
and everything therefore rests on the impaired matrix, which is where arm A's architecture is weakest (§6.4).

## 5. Arm A and str0m's SCTP sender — review-1 F1's criteria, answered

**F1's pass criterion: zero `Channel::write()` → `Ok(false)` in a 60 s 50 Mbps run.**

**PASS.** `arma-…-f-a60stock`, stock str0m 0.23.1, 60.0 s (3,600 frames queued at 60 fps), 50 Mbps offered:

| | |
|---|---|
| `Ok(false)` write refusals | **0** |
| polls in which any write was refused | **0** |
| frames dropped to backpressure | **0** (1 dropped before the channel opened) |
| peak buffered across all streams | **97,327 B** against the 131,072 B cap — 74% |
| fragments written | 192,751 video + 118,730 padding |
| carried | **49.6 Mbps** = 30.6 Mbps video + 19.0 Mbps synthetic padding |
| browser received | 371,657,432 B over 311,482 messages, `swoop-video` open throughout |

The patched build (`f-a60patched`, IW10 + RTO patches, 128 KiB cap retained) is the same answer: 0 refusals,
peak 111,267 B, 50.0 Mbps carried.

**Why F1's prediction did not materialise, stated so it is not mistaken for luck.** F1's arithmetic — "a
1080p IDR at 20 Mbps is routinely 200–400 KB… two frames in flight already exceed the cap" — is about
whole-frame messages. This arm fragments at **1200 bytes**, one SCTP DATA chunk at str0m's fixed
`INITIAL_MTU = 1228`, exactly as F2 instructed. The cap then bounds *in-flight bytes*, not *frames*, and
97 KB of in-flight bytes at 50 Mbps is 15.6 ms of buffer. The constant is unchanged and still hostile to a
whole-frame sender; the fragment size is what makes it survivable.

**Measured goodput ceiling, 0% loss, loopback** (the F1 remediation question). Offered load is raised with
`--dc-load`; the encoder cannot exceed ~31 Mbps on this box at any requested bitrate, so the balance is
synthetic padding the browser counts and discards. It is never folded into video goodput.

| offered | stock str0m | + IW10/RTO patches (128 KiB cap) | + 2 MB `MAX_BUFFERED_ACROSS_STREAMS` |
|---|---|---|---|
| 50 Mbps | 49.6 Mbps, 0 refusals | 49.8 Mbps, 0 refusals | 49.9 Mbps, 0 refusals |
| 100 Mbps | 92.9 Mbps, 10,404 refusals | 100.1 Mbps, 10,045 refusals | **42.3 Mbps**, 51,773 refusals, buffer pegged at 1,999,999 B |
| 200 Mbps | 156.5 Mbps, 357,115 refusals | 166.0 Mbps, 370,853 refusals | **43.3 Mbps**, 53,467 refusals, buffer pegged at 2,000,000 B |
| 400 Mbps | 163.1 Mbps, 364,131 refusals | — | — |

Each cell is one run of 19.8–21.9 s (1,188–1,314 frames at 60 fps) except the 50 Mbps stock cell, which is
the 60.0 s / 3,600-frame run above; `n` is the frame count, and the Mbps is bytes offered to the channel over
that window. **The loopback ceiling is ~163–166 Mbps**, refusals begin
between 50 and 100 Mbps, and they are transient (the arm re-offers the refused fragment on the next poll
rather than dropping the frame).

**Goodput at 1% and 2% loss: PENDING [human]** — §10. It is the row that matters, because 128 KiB of
in-flight data at a 40 ms RTT is a 26 Mbps window before any loss is involved, and `RTO_MIN` is 1000 ms.

**Single-frame-loss recovery time: PENDING [human]** — it requires injected loss and is therefore §10's.
The protocol is in §10.4. Nothing in this spike measured it, and no number for it exists.

## 6. The four SCTP patches — what each one measured

Carried as diffs in `bakeoff-host/sctp-patches/`, applied only through `--config .cargo/sctp-patched.toml`, so
the default build — and with it `cargo clippy -- -D warnings` and `cargo test` — stays on the released crates
and needs no `vendor/` directory.

1. **`sctp-proto.patch`** — initial cwnd 4380 B → IW10, post-RTO cwnd floor 1 MTU → 4 MTU. **Neutral on
   loopback**, which is the expected result: both only bite on loss or at a real RTT.
2. **`str0m-rto.patch`** — `RTO_INITIAL` 3000 → 500 ms, `RTO_MIN` 1000 → 400 ms, through sctp-proto's existing
   public setters, which str0m simply never calls. **Neutral on loopback**, same reason.
3. **`str0m-sendbuffer.patch`** — `MAX_BUFFERED_ACROSS_STREAMS` 128 KiB → 2 MB. **Measured harmful and left
   opt-in behind an environment variable**: 42.3 Mbps carried against a 100 Mbps offer with the buffer pegged
   at 1,999,999 B, versus 100.1 Mbps with the cap in place. sctp-proto has no pacer and no `max_burst`, so
   the extra room becomes a standing queue rather than throughput. **The 128 KiB cap is acting as an
   accidental and roughly correct latency bound**, and raising it without adding pacing makes the transport
   worse. This is the single most useful negative result in the spike.
4. The fourth file is `apply.sh`, which materialises `vendor/` from the local cargo registry so the repository
   carries diffs rather than 3.5 MB of somebody else's crate.

Patches 1 and 2 remain worth carrying into the impaired matrix — they are the two that are *designed* to bite
there and were correctly neutral here. Patch 3 should not be enabled without a pacer.

## 7. Congestion control is its own measurement — BWE on versus off

str0m installs its leaky-bucket pacer only when bandwidth estimation is enabled; with BWE off it uses a null
pacer and sends when told to. Both were measured on loopback in the same sitting, arm B, H.264, 20 Mbps
encoder target, n = 150 frames per latency row. **Pacer queue depth is read directly out of the library**
through `StreamTx::queue_info()` (`byte_size`, `packet_count`, and the age of `first_unsent`), sampled once
per recomputation — not inferred by subtracting a network baseline, which is what stages 1 and 2 had to do.

| | BWE **off** (null pacer) | BWE **on** (leaky bucket, desired = 3× encoder target) |
|---|---|---|
| `_rv` p50 / p95 (n = 150) | **15.3 / 30.7 ms** | **1165.9 / 1465.7 ms** |
| `pushed → arrival` p50 / p95 (n = 150) | 1.4 / 2.7 ms | **1155.8 / 1450.1 ms** |
| host `present → pushed` p50 / p95 (n = 150) | 8.1 / 9.4 ms | 8.4 / 10.1 ms |
| **pacer queue delay** p50 / p95 / max | **0.0 / 0.0 / 0.0 ms** (n = 3,666) | **1015.6 / 1437.3 / 1521.0 ms** (n = 4,803) |
| **pacer queue depth** p50 / max | 0 B / 41,102 B, 0 / 37 packets | **916,788 B / 1,633,528 B, 861 / 1,498 packets** |
| delivered frame rate | 59 fps | 46 fps |
| `packetsLost` / `pliCount` / `nackCount` / `freezeCount` | 0 / 0 / 0 / 0 | **0 / 0 / 0 / 0** |
| jitter buffer delay per frame | 0.7 ms | 20.7 ms |
| last bandwidth estimate | n/a | **8.9 Mbps** |

Four things follow, and they are D4 findings, not D3 findings.

1. **The pacer queue is the latency.** 1015.6 ms of queue inside a 1165.9 ms end-to-end figure — 87% of it.
   The host's own half of the pipeline is unchanged (8.1 vs 8.4 ms p50). Stage 1's "1247 ms p50 of pacer
   queue" was inferred; this is the queue itself, measured in the library, in bytes, packets and
   milliseconds.
2. **It presents as latency, never as loss.** Zero lost packets, zero PLIs, zero NACKs, zero freezes, zero
   dropped frames in `getStats` — in both columns. A monitoring story built on loss counters would report this
   configuration as perfectly healthy while the viewer watches a second-old desktop.
3. **GoogCC settled at 8.9 Mbps on loopback** — a path with no capacity limit at all, against a 20 Mbps
   encoder and a 60 Mbps desired bitrate. It read its own standing queue as congestion. Whatever the cause,
   a bandwidth estimator that converges to 45% of the source rate on an unimpaired path is not ready to
   govern the encoder, and plan.md D14's per-viewer uplink budget is built on exactly that signal.
4. **Therefore every latency row in this memo is measured with BWE off**, and that is disclosed rather than
   hidden: a bake-off row that is 87% pacer queue measures the pacer, not the video path. It also means
   **this spike has not measured congestion control at all** — it has measured that str0m's, as configured
   here, is not usable yet. The step-response experiments research/05 §7 asks for (50 → 5 → 50 Mbps, time to
   first cut, overshoot, recovery) are not run and no number for them exists.

Arm A is not affected by any of this: it negotiates no media m-line, so str0m's estimator never runs. That is
also arm A's gap — it has **no congestion control of its own**, which is a v1 obligation, not a spike one.

## 8. ICE restart — works, measured

plan.md D13 promotes a relayed session to a direct one by ICE restart, so this had to be demonstrated rather
than asserted. `pipeline.rs` owns the live sink on its own thread and may not be modified, so the re-offer is
parked in a one-slot mailbox (`sink::Reoffer`), the sink drains it inside its own `poll` on the thread that
owns the `Rtc`, and `POST /reoffer` waits for the answer. The browser calls `pc.restartIce()`, re-offers, and
applies the answer.

`armb-…-icerestart`, arm B, same machine, one restart on a live peer carrying 60 fps of H.264:

| | before | after |
|---|---|---|
| local ICE ufrag (browser) | `nibP` | `S4NB` |
| remote ICE ufrag (host) | `4uPGxPvH9SvFQiZr` | `1S8yYRzKRgbtqcZ8` |
| selected candidate pair id | `CPpWA98c27_JJP7EPCZ` | `CP9pvy9ztW_IdXgo4je` |
| client's local candidate port | 58109 | **57320** — the browser re-gathered |
| host's UDP port (`getStats` remote candidate) | 58111 | **58111** — unchanged |
| `iceConnectionState` | `connected` | `connected` |

Both sides minted new credentials (`credentialsChanged: true`), the candidate pair was renominated, and the

## 10. The impaired matrix — PENDING [human]. What clumsy can and cannot express

### 10.1 The tool

**clumsy 0.3** — the version string in the binary is literally `clumsy 0.3`; `clumsy.exe` is dated
2022-01-12 and carries no version resource. It ships `WinDivert.dll` and `WinDivert64.sys`, both dated
2019-10-20, the driver reporting **WinDivert 2.2** in its version resource. Installed at
`C:\Users\admin\AppData\Local\Microsoft\WinGet\Packages\Jagt.Clumsy_Microsoft.Winget.Source_8wekyb3d8bbwe\clumsy.exe`.

It requires elevation because it installs and starts the WinDivert kernel driver; the binary's own string is
*"clumsy needs to be elevated to work. Run as Administrator or click Yes in promoted UAC dialog"*. **The owner
launches it from an elevated console by deliberate click. No agent path in this project elevates anything**,
and none did here — every fact in this section was read out of the binary and its `config.txt`, not by running
it.

Its modules, read from the executable's own option and label strings: **lag** (`--lag-time`, "Delay(ms)"),
**drop** (`--drop-chance`, "Chance(%)"), **throttle** (`--throttle-chance`, `--throttle-frame`,
"Timeframe(ms)", "Drop Throttled"), **duplicate**, **out of order** (`--ood-chance`), **tamper**,
**set TCP RST** and **bandwidth** ("Limit(KB/s)"). Each takes `--<module>-inbound` / `--<module>-outbound`.

### 10.2 What it cannot express

**There is no jitter module.** `lag` applies a **single fixed delay** to every matched packet; there is no
variance, no distribution and no parameter for one. The task asks for 2% loss, 40 ms RTT **and 10 ms jitter**,
and **clumsy 0.3 cannot produce the jitter term**. Two things come close and neither is it:

- **`throttle --throttle-frame 10`** holds matched packets for a 10 ms window and releases them as a group.
  The added delay per packet sweeps roughly 0–10 ms, but it is strongly correlated within each group — a burst
  release, not an independent per-packet perturbation — and it destroys the inter-packet spacing that a
  bandwidth estimator reads. Describing it as "10 ms jitter" would be false.
- **`ood --ood-chance N`** repositions a packet within the buffer. That is reordering, not a delay
  distribution.

**Recommendation: run the matrix as 2% loss + 40 ms RTT with the jitter cell explicitly unproduced, and label
it that way in the memo and in the run JSON.** Do not substitute throttle and call it jitter. If the jitter
term is judged load-bearing for G1 — it is not obviously so, since the D3 rule names only loss and RTT — the
tool that expresses it properly is Linux `tc netem delay 20ms 5ms distribution normal` on a box placed in the
path between the two machines, which is a hardware request, not a software one.

Two further constraints, both from the binary's own strings: **only one instance of clumsy may run at a time**
("There's already an instance of clumsy running"), and for loopback captures the filter **cannot include
inbound criteria** ("When capturing localhost (loopback) packets, you CAN'T include inbound criteria"). The
second does not bite here because the impaired matrix runs on the LAN row, not on loopback.

### 10.3 The parameters, exactly

Run clumsy **on the host**, filtering **UDP only**:

```
Filter:  udp and (ip.DstAddr == <CLIENT-IP> or ip.SrcAddr == <CLIENT-IP>)

Lag      : on, inbound + outbound, Delay(ms) = 20      -> 40 ms RTT (20 ms each leg)
Drop     : on, inbound + outbound, Chance(%) = 2.0     -> the 2% row
           (Chance = 0 for the 0% row, 1.0 for the 1% row)
Jitter   : NOT PRODUCED — clumsy 0.3 has no jitter module (§10.2)
Throttle : off      Duplicate: off      Out of order: off      Tamper: off      Bandwidth: off
```

Equivalent command line, for the owner's elevated console:

```
clumsy.exe --filter "udp and (ip.DstAddr == 192.168.88.20 or ip.SrcAddr == 192.168.88.20)" ^
           --lag on --lag-time 20 --lag-inbound 1 --lag-outbound 1 ^
           --drop on --drop-chance 2.0 --drop-inbound 1 --drop-outbound 1
```

(substitute the client's real address for `192.168.88.20`).

**The `udp` term in the filter is load-bearing and must not be widened to the whole host.** The QPC ↔
`performance.now()` clock exchange runs over **TCP 17441** to the same machine. Impairing it would inflate the
exchange's measured round trip from 0.6 ms to ~40 ms and take the offset's uncertainty from ±0.25 ms to
roughly ±20 ms, which would make every cross-clock figure in the impaired row meaningless — including the
`_rv` row itself. Impair the media, never the instrument.

### 10.4 What the impaired matrix must produce

Run each cell three times, n ≥ 150 frames, arms A / B / C, and for arm A all four `dcmode` values.

1. `_rv` p50 and p95 per arm at 0%, 1%, 2% loss with 40 ms RTT — Table 1 row 2, and D3's second clause.
2. **Goodput per arm** at each loss rate, and for arm A the `Ok(false)` count and peak buffered bytes at each
   (§5's table repeated under impairment, which is where the 128 KiB cap becomes a 26 Mbps window at 40 ms RTT
   and where `RTO_MIN` finally bites).
3. **Single-frame-loss recovery time.** Drop a burst with `--drop-chance` briefly at 100%, or use research/05
   §7's 20-packet burst, and measure wall-clock from the drop to the first correctly rendered frame. Arm B and
   C recover through PLI → IDR; arm A recovers through its own `frameId`-gap detection → `idr` request → forced
   IDR. Report the three separately: they are three different mechanisms.
4. The reliability-mode matrix (Table 4) re-run under loss. **This is the only condition under which it can
   produce an answer**, and review-1 F2 called it "the single highest-information experiment in the wave".
5. Whether patches 1 and 2 (§6) stop being neutral. They are designed to bite exactly here.

## 13. G1 recommendation — **awaiting owner sign-off**

### 13.1 The honest answer first

**G1 cannot be decided on the data in this memo.** The row the amended D3 rule is evaluated on — the real LAN
hop — does not exist, and the clause most likely to decide between the two surviving arms — "does not lose to
B at 2% loss / 40 ms RTT" — is unmeasured. Recommending a transport now would mean recommending it on
same-machine rows that 0.1 §2.2 forbids quoting as product latencies, with the adversarial half of the rule
skipped. §9 and §10 are half a day of the owner's time, and they are the half that decides.

### 13.2 What the measurements do support

**Arm C is out.** It loses to B on p50 by 3.7 ms, and its p95 advantage survives against B's median but not
against B's best observed p95 (4.1 ms margin, against the 15 ms the amended rule requires). The mechanism is
settled and will not improve: the receive-side `RTCRtpScriptTransform` sits **after** Chrome's frame buffer —
`push → transform` 10.7 / 17.0 ms against the identical sender's `push → arrival` of 1.4 / 2.8 ms into a
`<video>` — so it cannot buy the jitter buffer back. research/02 §4.6's open question is answered and the arm
it was asked about does not pay for itself. It should be dropped from G1 and, if anyone wants it later, it is
the Wave 9 "second video path" track.

**Between A and B, the ranking rows favour A, and they favour it on the tail rather than the median.**
Arm A's `_rv*` p95 is 11.1 ms against arm B's 63.1 ms in the same sitting, and against **every one of the nine
arm B p95 figures measured on this box** (30.7–82.1 ms) arm A clears the amended rule's 15 ms margin. Arm A's
p95/p50 ratio is 1.1; arm B's is 4.1. The difference is entirely `arrival → composition` inside Chrome, with
zero packet loss and zero PLIs, i.e. it is Chrome's render scheduling for a `<video>` element and not anything
str0m or the encoder is doing.

**Arm A's predicted blocker did not materialise.** review-1 F1 rated str0m's SCTP sender a critical risk;
measured, it carried 49.6 Mbps for 60 s with **zero** `Channel::write()` → `Ok(false)`, peaking at 74% of the
128 KiB cap, and its loopback ceiling is ~163–166 Mbps. The reason is the 1200-byte fragment, and F1's
arithmetic was about whole-frame messages.

**But arm A's risk is concentrated exactly where the measurement is missing.** It has no NACK/RTX, no FEC and
no congestion control of its own; at a 40 ms RTT the 128 KiB shared buffer is a 26 Mbps window before any loss,
and `RTO_MIN` is 1000 ms in the released crate. Arm B gets NACK/RTX, TWCC and a jitter buffer for free. If the
impaired row goes against arm A, it will go against it decisively, and the amended rule's second clause is a
hard AND, not a tie-break.

### 13.3 The provisional recommendation

**Provisionally, arm A on str0m — conditional on §10.** With the explicit fallback written down now, before
the data exists, so it cannot be argued after:

- If arm A holds at 2% loss / 40 ms RTT — i.e. it does not lose to B on `_rv` p50 or p95 and its goodput does
  not collapse — **take arm A, and D4 stays str0m 0.23.1** with patches 1 and 2 (§6) and without patch 3.
- If arm A loses under impairment, **take arm B**, and arm B's p95 becomes a *task*, not a reason to pick A:
  the tail is inside Chrome between arrival and composition on a stream with zero loss, and the next things to
  try are the playout-delay `max` value (100 ms today, the range is (0, 500]), `requestVideoFrameCallback`
  scheduling, and whether `desynchronized` presentation helps. None of those has been tried, and the amended
  rule's job is to make sure that work gets scheduled rather than silently accepted.
- Either way **arm C is out**, and the seam stays — `sink::VideoSink` on the host, `receivers/receiver.js` in
  the browser — because it cost two arms and three branches in `httpd.rs` to prove it works and it is what
  makes a later reversal cheap.

### 13.4 Carried forward to D4 (the transport library), not D3

These are findings about str0m rather than about the video path, and they land on Tasks 1.2, 3.8 and 4.7
whichever arm wins.

1. **str0m's pacer is not usable as configured.** BWE on, desired bitrate at 3× the encoder target: 1015.6 ms
   p50 of pacer queue (n = 4,803 snapshots, peak 1,633,528 bytes / 1,498 packets), a bandwidth estimate that
   settled at 8.9 Mbps on loopback, 46 of 60 fps delivered — with zero loss, zero PLIs, zero NACKs and zero
   freezes. It presents as latency and never as loss, so no loss-based health check will catch it. Every
   latency row in this memo is measured with BWE **off**, which means the product still needs a working
   congestion controller and does not have one.
2. **The SCTP ceiling is ~163–166 Mbps on loopback**, and `MAX_BUFFERED_ACROSS_STREAMS` at 128 KiB is doing
   useful work: raising it to 2 MB **dropped carried throughput to 42.3 Mbps** with the buffer pegged, because
   sctp-proto has no pacer and no `max_burst`. **The `str0m-sendbuffer.patch` is measured harmful and is left
   opt-in behind `SWOOP_SCTP_SENDBUFFER=1`.** Do not enable it without adding pacing. The cap is shared with
   every other channel on the association, so **Task 6.2's clipboard size cap is a number derived from this
   constant**, not a round guess — review-1 F1's point, still standing.
3. **str0m 0.23.1 accepts a remote-initiated ICE restart** and keeps its local candidates through it (§8),
   which is what plan.md D13's relay→direct promotion depends on.
4. **`wincrypto` does not interoperate with Chrome 153** — every DTLS handshake fails decryption.
   `wincrypto-dimpl` works first time and is where str0m is heading (PR #1038 removes SChannel outright). This
   is a pin with a reason and an exit condition, and it belongs in Task 1.2's `Cargo.toml` comment.
5. **MSRV 1.91.0, verified**, set by `moq-nvenc 0.0.5`, not by str0m (1.85.0).
